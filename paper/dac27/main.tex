\title{Kairos: Schedule Exploration for Multicore RTL Simulation}

\begin{abstract}
Cycle-accurate RTL simulation of a multicore is deterministic: a test
program explores exactly one interleaving, no matter how many times it
is run. Silicon does not have this limitation---timing jitter is why
post-silicon multicore validation works at all. We measure the resulting
coverage wall: reverting a real dual-Unique coherence bug changes
nothing observable on five directed reproducers, because the schedule
never varies. \emph{Kairos} restores schedule diversity pre-silicon by
delaying harts under a reproducible policy. The RTL cost is one bit per
core gating an existing handshake; compiled out, the generated netlist
is identical to the original design (0 differing lines). Because a stall
is always architecturally legal, every induced schedule is one the
hardware could reach unaided---there is no false-positive class from the
mechanism. On a quad-core out-of-order RISC-V, eight runs of a
conventional regression explore one interleaving; the same eight runs
under Kairos explore up to eight. Two latent races in the shared SMP
boot code---missed by years of green CI---were found in under ten runs
of workloads below 7{,}000 cycles and reduced to one named-hart delay.
Both are software races, not RTL bugs.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A cycle-accurate RTL simulator is a pure function of its inputs. Given
the same binary, the same reset, and the same device tree, a multicore
simulation produces the same interleaving of memory events on every
run. Repeating a regression a thousand times therefore explores one
schedule, not a thousand.

Silicon does not have this problem. Clock jitter, interrupt arrival,
DRAM refresh, voltage droop and thermal throttling re-order the machine
on every execution, which is why post-silicon multicore validation
keeps finding coherence and memory-ordering bugs that pre-silicon
simulation ran past~\cite{hangal2004tsotool,lee2017mtracecheck}. That
variation is a \emph{coverage source}. Simulation throws it away
precisely at the stage where bugs are cheapest to fix.

The gap is not hypothetical. Five directed reproducers for a dual-Unique
coherence violation in the processor studied here---two L1s holding the
same line Unique at once---passed on both the buggy and the fixed RTL.
The only known activation was an 871-million-cycle Linux boot. Measured
directly: with the fix reverted, all five workloads produced
\emph{bit-identical} cache tag-transition counts to the fixed model
(Section~\ref{sec:wall}). Removing a real coherence bug changed
nothing observable, because the schedule never varied.

This paper treats that determinism as a coverage wall and asks whether
it can be lifted without putting a Linux-booting design at risk.
\emph{Kairos} delays harts according to a controlled, reproducible
policy so that one test program samples many interleavings. The RTL
change is one input per core, gating a handshake the frontend already
de-asserts on every I-cache miss (Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}). Tied low, or
compiled out, the design is the original design---measured, not
asserted. Because a core is always permitted to stall, every schedule
Kairos induces is a schedule the unmodified hardware could reach, and
any failure it finds is a real failure.

The ideas are imported from software concurrency
testing~\cite{musuvathi2007chess,musuvathi2008chess,burckhardt2010pct}
and the paper is generous about that debt. What does not transfer---no
blocking signal, a scheduling-point count in the hundreds of thousands,
a livelock oracle that must discount the tool's own stalls---is
measured, and is the interesting part of the work.

This paper makes four contributions:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The determinism wall, measured.} Eight runs of a
  conventional RTL regression explore one interleaving, on every
  workload we ran (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}a, Figure~\ref{fig:explore}).
  Reverting a real coherence bug does not change a single tag
  transition (Section~\ref{sec:wall}).
\item \textbf{A sound perturbation.} One bit per core, four lines of
  logic, netlist-identical when compiled out. Stalling cannot manufacture
  a state the design could not reach unaided.
\item \textbf{A four-policy ablation on one DUT.} Deterministic
  regression, UVM-style random delay, PCT, and windowed delay: 96
  runs, two coverage metrics, time to first finding
  (Section~\ref{sec:eval}).
\item \textbf{Two reduced, replayable findings} in boot code that
  every SMP test in the repository links. Both survived years of a
  green deterministic regression. Both are software races, not RTL
  bugs; stating that is part of the result.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Program fuzzers for RTL.}
RFUZZ~\cite{laeufer2018rfuzz} mutates mux inputs under mux-toggle
coverage. DifuzzRTL~\cite{hur2021difuzzrtl} guides on control-register

MorFuzz~\cite{xu2023morfuzz} reports 19 bugs. All of these
evaluations are single-core. None controls the interleaving of a
coherent multicore, and none checks live cache state or stall-aware
liveness.

\paragraph{Multicore oracles and MCM testers.}
DiffTest~\cite{xu2022xiangshan} and DiffTest-H~\cite{ma2025difftesth}
are commit-level lockstep, including multicore; DiffTest-H reports 151
complex bugs in XiangShan. That is oracle layer~1 here, and we keep
it. It is silent on hangs and on illegal microarchitectural states
that have not yet corrupted a commit---the dual-Unique bug was a
Linux hang $\sim$10$^8$ cycles later. TSOtool~\cite{hangal2004tsotool}
and McVerSi~\cite{elver2016mcversi} vary the \emph{program} and check
values against a memory model, on silicon or gem5, where timing
already varies. McVerSi found 2 new gem5 bugs and all 11 studied bugs
in one day, against 2/11 for litmus tests. MTraceCheck~\cite{lee2017mtracecheck}
is post-silicon. Litmus tests~\cite{alglave2014herding} enumerate
allowed outcomes. Kairos is the complement: fix the program, vary the
schedule, observe the microarchitecture, minimise the result.

\paragraph{Software schedule exploration.}
CHESS~\cite{musuvathi2007chess,musuvathi2008chess} and
PCT~\cite{burckhardt2010pct} explore thread schedules; delta
debugging~\cite{zeller2002ddmin} minimises failing inputs. The ideas
are theirs. Section~\ref{sec:design} states what breaks in hardware.
UVM testbenches randomise interface delays~\cite{ieee1800uvm} with no
coverage notion; Kairos controls the cores.

\section{The Determinism Wall}
\label{sec:wall}

The strongest single argument in this paper was produced as a
by-product of a failed experiment. A continuous SWMR probe over all
four L1 tag arrays was pointed at five SMP workloads, once on the
fixed RTL and once with the dual-Unique fix reverted. Tag-state
transition counts over 6 million cycles per workload were
\emph{identical to the digit}: 4{,}091 / 4{,}158 / 26{,}090 /
160{,}148 / 139{,}716 on \texttt{mt-fencei}, \texttt{mt-crosscall},
\texttt{mt-llist}, \texttt{mt-seqlock}, \texttt{mt-lrsc}. The programs
were not wrong; each ran one schedule, and that schedule did not
contain the race. The Linux boot found the bug by brute force at
$\sim$8.7$\times$10$^8$ cycles---coverage by accident.

The same wall appears as a coverage number. Under the deterministic
policy, eight runs of each of three SMP micros produce one distinct
interleaving (Figure~\ref{fig:explore}, top row of each panel).
Novelty rate is $1/8$. That is what a conventional RTL regression does
today, written as a fraction.

\section{Kairos}
\label{sec:design}

Kairos is a host-side scheduler around a one-bit RTL hook
(Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}). Everything above the DUT boundary---policies,
coverage, oracles, reduction, replay---is written against an
eleven-method interface (\mbox{\texttt{dut.h}}). Porting to another multicore
RTL model means implementing that interface and adding one stall bit
per hart.

\subsection{Perturbation and soundness}
\label{sec:soundness}

The RTL change is one input per core, \texttt{scheduleStall}, ANDed
into the fetch$\rightarrow$decode handshake (Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}a). Holding it high is behaviourally identical to fetch not being
ready, a condition the design already produces on every I-cache miss.
So:

\begin{itemize}
\item A stall can only \emph{delay} a hart. It cannot skip work, force
  progress, win an arbitration, or change a value.
\item Every induced schedule is one the unmodified design could reach
  on its own. A failure under Kairos is a failure of the design (or of
  the software running on it), not an artefact of the tool. There is
  no false-positive class from the mechanism.
\item With the mask held at zero the design behaves as it does without
  Kairos. With the feature compiled out, the generated Verilog is
  identical to a build that never heard of Kairos.
\end{itemize}

That last claim took four tries: Chisel derives identifiers from
elaboration order, so a refactor that looks equivalent is not
netlist-neutral. Writing the disabled path as the literal original
statement is the only form that gives 0 differing lines. This
separates the mechanism from fault injection: a stall produces only
states the silicon could reach.

\subsection{Policies}
\label{sec:policies}

A policy decides, each cycle, which harts to hold. Every policy is a
pure function of $(\textit{seed}, \textit{cycle}, \textit{progress})$---no
wall-clock, no I/O---so a finding is reproducible from its seed alone.
Four policies ship, and three of them exist to be argued with:

\begin{itemize}
\item \texttt{deterministic} never stalls. It is the control: what a
  conventional regression does today.
\item \texttt{random}$(p)$ holds each hart independently with
  probability $p$ per cycle. This is the honest strawman---roughly
  what a UVM testbench does with randomised delays~\cite{ieee1800uvm}.
  If it matches PCT, PCT is not earning its complexity.
\item \texttt{pct}$(d)$ is PCT~\cite{burckhardt2010pct} transferred to
  hardware: random hart priorities plus $d{-}1$ random priority-change
  points, with bounded leadership tenure (below).
\item \texttt{windowed}$(w)$ holds one hart for one contiguous window.
  Wide races (a walker writeback outliving a peer's refill) need a
  contiguous delay; per-cycle coin flips reopen and reclose them
  immediately.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What does not transfer from PCT.}
Software PCT runs the highest-priority enabled thread; the
runtime knows when a thread blocks. Hardware has no such signal, and
``has not retired'' is not a substitute: a hart spinning on a flag
retires forever and would hold the machine forever. Measured, before
bounded tenure: \texttt{pct:3} left two harts with zero retired
instructions after 662{,}901 cycles. Leadership therefore expires
after 4{,}096 cycles. This adds priority changes the original
guarantee does not account for, and is stated rather than hidden.

Three further caveats, all load-bearing. (1)~In software, $k$ is the
number of scheduling steps; in hardware every cycle is one, so $k$ is
far larger and the bound correspondingly weaker. (2)~The depth of a
coherence race is not obviously the same notion as the depth of a data
race. (3)~Kairos transfers PCT's \emph{structure} and measures how the
bound behaves; it does not inherit the guarantee.

\paragraph{Calibration, not assumption.}
A policy has to know \emph{when} to perturb. Spreading delays over the
cycle \emph{budget} is wrong whenever the budget is a safety cap rather
than a prediction. The first working build placed one delay window
uniformly over a 400{,}000-cycle budget for a 6{,}629-cycle workload,
so in $\sim$98\% of runs the window landed after the program had
already ended. Six runs, six identical digests, a campaign that looked
perfectly healthy while exploring nothing. Kairos now measures the
unperturbed run length first and spreads over that. The same run is
the control: if the workload does not pass unperturbed, the campaign
refuses to start.

\subsection{Oracles}
\label{sec:oracles}

Four layers run on every cycle of every run. Each answers a different
question, and each is reported separately.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Result.} Did the workload compute the right answer?
\item \textbf{Liveness.} Hang: a hart stops retiring.
  Livelock: every hart retires and the workload never finishes. Both
  produce no values, so layer~1 and every value-based multicore oracle
  in the literature are structurally blind to them. Four of the
  hardest bugs in this project's history were hangs.
\item \textbf{Structural.} SWMR on the live L1 tag arrays, every
  cycle~\cite{sorin2011primer}. Fires at the transaction, not at the
  symptom $\sim$10$^8$ cycles later.
\item \textbf{Architectural.} Committed state versus a golden model,
  excluding legitimately racy commits. The strongest oracle when a
  reference exists.
\end{enumerate}

Liveness and perturbation interact, and getting this wrong invalidates
every result: a hart Kairos is deliberately holding is \emph{supposed}
to stop retiring. Both liveness layers therefore count only
\emph{unstalled} cycles. A livelock verdict additionally requires that
no hart has been held for more than $1/8$ of the elapsed run; past
that the run is a \texttt{timeout}, which is not a finding. Without
that discount the first four-policy sweep reported seven livelock
``bugs'', all of them Kairos holding harts. The soundness argument
constrains which \emph{states} a stall can produce; it says nothing
about what an \emph{oracle} may infer from a run the tool itself
stalled into silence.

Known limitation: PCT stalls all but one hart, so on four harts every
hart is held far more than $1/8$ of any run and the livelock layer
cannot fire under it. PCT contributes structural and wrong-result
findings, not liveness ones. That is the price of a rule chosen never
to cry wolf.

\subsection{Coverage}
\label{sec:coverage}

Two metrics, sensitive to different things.

\emph{Distinct schedules} counts 64-bit digests over the ordered
sequence of cross-hart memory events. The digest excludes retirements
and cycle numbers: two runs that produce the same event order at
different absolute times are the same interleaving, and counting them
separately is exactly the mistake a naive ``hash the whole trace''
metric makes.

\emph{Order pairs} are the observed $(\textit{hart }A\textit{ touched
line }L\textit{ before hart }B)$ orderings. Coarser, harder to saturate
by accident, and the number to look at when someone asks whether
``distinct schedules'' is doing real work. Section~\ref{sec:eval}
shows PCT leading on the first metric and losing on the second.

\subsection{Reduction and replay}
\label{sec:reduce}

A campaign that finds a bug hands over a schedule holding harts
thousands of times across millions of cycles. Kairos reduces it.
The failing run is re-recorded as stall spans
$[\textit{start},\textit{end})\ \textit{mask}$. Delta
debugging~\cite{zeller2002ddmin} drops spans until no remaining span
can be dropped without losing the failure; a second pass binary-searches
each surviving span from both ends. The predicate is ``does this still
reproduce the \emph{same} verdict'', not merely ``does this still
fail''---reducing a hang into a wrong-result failure would silently
retarget the reduction onto a different bug.

Delta debugging normally struggles with concurrency because the
predicate is flaky. Here it is not: the DUT is a cycle-accurate
simulator and the candidate schedule is fully specified, so the
predicate is a pure function. The same determinism that makes RTL
simulation blind to interleavings is what makes reduction over them
exact. The output is a five-line \texttt{.ksched} file that replays
on any host, after the RTL changes, without a seed.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\paragraph{Setup.}
Chiron is a 4-core RV64IMA out-of-order processor with AXI-ACE
snoops, private L1s and a shared L2, simulated under Verilator.
Kairos links the fast (no-trace, threaded) model. Workloads are the
in-tree SMP micros. Campaigns calibrate against an unperturbed run and
refuse to start if that run does not pass. Self-tests of the
design-independent half assert 100{,}052 checks against a mock DUT in
one second, and are mutation-tested against nine deliberate regressions.

\subsection{Does a deterministic simulator explore one interleaving?}
\label{sec:one}

Yes. Figure~\ref{fig:explore} audits the claim run by run. Four
policies, three workloads, eight seeds (96 runs). The

simulator, not of a particular binary.

Random delay is a weak strawman on short workloads: $1/8$ distinct on
\texttt{mt-illegal}, competitive only on the 390\,K-cycle workload,
where a 2\%-per-cycle coin flip has 390{,}000 cycles to land somewhere
useful. Per-cycle randomisation targets nothing.

PCT explores most and finds least, and the reason belongs in the text
rather than a footnote. It leads or ties on distinct schedules
everywhere---but on two of the three workloads it never finished the
program: holding three of four harts costs $\sim$100$\times$ in run
length, so 16 of its 24 runs ended at the budget and those digests are
of partial executions. Its order-pair count is also \emph{lower} on
\texttt{mt-crosscall}, 48 against 144, because serialising harts visits
many schedules without visiting many orderings. A single
distinct-schedule number would have shown PCT winning three times over.
That is an argument about the coverage model, not against PCT.

Windowed delay found both bugs: one contiguous delay of one named
hart, the corner of the schedule space both findings lived in.

\paragraph{The experiment that did not work.}
We do not claim a $10^3$--$10^4\times$ improvement on the dual-Unique
bug, and we ran the experiment that would have supported it. On the
buggy RTL of Section~\ref{sec:wall}, rebuilt with the hook, all four
policies over \texttt{mt-fencei} and \texttt{mt-crosscall}, 12 seeds
each, gave \textbf{96 runs and 0 findings}---while PCT reached 12/12
distinct schedules and windowed 8/12. Diversity went up; the bug still
did not fire within $\sim$10$^6$ cycles, against the
$8.7{\times}10^8$ the Linux boot needed. That measured negative is the
evidence for Section~\ref{sec:limit}: a whole-core stall delays
everything a hart does, and this race needs one walker writeback to
outlive one peer's refill of one line.

\paragraph{Finding 2 --- a latent race in the project's own boot code.}
Found on \texttt{mt-illegal} (baseline 1{,}404 cycles) under
\texttt{windowed:400}, shrunk to hart~2 held for 220 cycles at
cycle 284. All four harts spin forever in \texttt{crt.S}: every hart
stores 1 to \texttt{hart\_init\_sync}, hart~0 later stores 0 to
release everyone, and nothing orders a slow hart's store against that
release. If any hart is delayed past hart~0's clear, its store re-arms
the flag and every hart waits forever. The register dump is
conclusive: \texttt{a2 = 1} on all four harts, including hart~0, the
hart that executed the clearing store.

This defect is in code every SMP test in the repository links. It
survived years of a green deterministic regression because a
deterministic simulator always produces the one interleaving in which
hart~0's \texttt{.bss} loop happens to take long enough. It was found
in four runs of a 1{,}404-cycle workload.

\paragraph{Finding 1 --- three compounding bugs around a barrier.}
Figure~\ref{fig:finding} is the anatomy. Under \texttt{windowed:2000},
seed~3, delaying hart~2 leaves a 6{,}629-cycle workload still running
at 662{,}901 cycles with all four harts retiring inside their first
barrier. The shrinker reduced 1{,}181 hart-cycles of delay to 343, in
20 simulation runs (Figure~\ref{fig:finding}b). The cause was three
defects stacked: the linker script did not cover \texttt{.sbss}, so
the shared barrier state sat past \texttt{\_ebss} and was never
zeroed; every hart ``initialised'' it with an unsynchronised store at
thread entry; and that store was an 8-byte \texttt{sd} on an
\texttt{int *}, clobbering the adjacent sense flag. After the fix,
replaying the same 343-cycle schedule on the new binary completes in
8{,}213 cycles with all four harts at \texttt{exit}.

\paragraph{Attribution discipline.}
Kairos reports ``this schedule wedges the machine''. Triage decides
whether the fault is in the hardware or the software. The project's
own history says why that matters: \texttt{mt-llist} spent weeks as a
suspected coherence bug before turning out to be its own CAS loop
fencing itself into a livelock. We do not count either finding as an
RTL bug, and we do not inflate the finding count with timeouts.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limit}

Whole-core stalls are too coarse for at least one line-level race,
and we measured that rather than suspecting it: 96 exploring runs on
the RTL with the dual-Unique fix reverted produced no finding
(Section~\ref{sec:compare}). That bug needs a walker writeback to
outlive a peer's refill of a particular line; stalling a hart delays
everything that hart does. Optional next knobs---snoop-response delay,
arbiter priority---act at the transaction rather than the core, are
equally sound by the same argument, and are not evaluated here. PCT's bug-depth guarantee does not transfer cleanly
(Section~\ref{sec:policies}), and PCT cannot contribute liveness
findings under the conservative oracle rule. The evaluation is one
DUT. Timeouts are not findings; a campaign that under-provisions its
budget reports nothing, which is the honest outcome.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conc}

RTL simulation of a multicore explores one interleaving per program.
We measured that wall, on a real coherence bug that five directed
tests could not distinguish from its fix, and we lifted it with one
bit per core and a host-side scheduler. The failures are real because
a stall is always legal; the disabled design is the original design
because the netlist matches to the line; the findings replay because
the DUT is deterministic and the schedule is a file. Schedule
exploration is not a better fuzzer. It is the other axis.
